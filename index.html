What is the Coefficient of Restitution?

The Coefficient of Restitution (e) is a dimensionless parameter quantifying the elasticity of a collision between two bodies. It represents the ratio of the final relative separation velocity to the initial relative approach velocity.

\[e = \frac{v_{\text{separation}}}{u_{\text{approach}}}\]

A value of \(e = 1\) represents a perfectly elastic collision with no kinetic energy loss. A value of \(e = 0\) represents a perfectly plastic collision where objects stick. Natural materials fall in between (\(0 < e < 1\)).

Theoretical Framework

Review the governing equations and experimental procedure used to derive the restitution coefficient before entering the laboratory.

Impulse-Momentum & Restitution

The Coefficient of Restitution (e) is fundamentally derived from the impulse-momentum principle as the ratio of relative separation velocity to relative approach velocity for two colliding bodies A and B:

\[v_B' - v_A' = e(v_A - v_B)\]

For a direct central impact with a surface of "infinite mass" (like the ground, where \(v_B = v_B' = 0\)), this simplifies to:

\[v_A' = -e \cdot v_A\]

Meaning the rebound velocity magnitude is \(e\) times the impact velocity magnitude. This simulation explicitly models this ground-impact case.

Boundary cases: \(e=0\) represents a perfectly plastic impact (objects stick, energy lost), while \(e=1\) represents a perfectly elastic impact (no energy loss). Real materials fall between these extremes.

Kinematics of the Drop

By applying Torricelli's equation (\(v^2 = u^2 + 2gh\)), we find the initial impact velocity \(v_0 = \sqrt{2gh_0}\). Over repeated bounces, velocity decays exponentially with each impact:

\[v_n = e^n \cdot v_0\]

Because kinetic energy relates to velocity squared, and height relates linearly to energy (\(PE = mgh\)), the spatial height decays corresponding to the square of \(e\):

\[h_n = h_0 \cdot e^{2n}\]

These two relations—velocity and height—are two views of the same underlying physical kinematic dissipation.

Measuring 'e' Experimentally

While this simulation uses an idealized environment (omitting air resistance), real physical laboratories rely on dropping specimens and recording their motion paths via sensors or high-speed cameras.

By measuring the experimental ratio of initial drop height (\(h\)) and rebound height (\(h'\)) after \(n\) bounces, we can reverse-engineer the true physical \(e\) value without directly measuring complex energy dissipation. The height-based relation rearranged provides a practical methodology:

\[e = \left(\frac{h'}{h}\right)^{\frac{1}{2n}}\]

History & Definitions of e

The coefficient of restitution was first formulated by Sir Isaac Newton in 1687 as an experimental law: the relative speed of separation of two bodies after collision is proportional to their relative speed of approach before it. The constant of proportionality is e. Newton's law is phenomenological—it lets us predict post-collision velocities without knowing the detailed forces during the impact.

Important property: e belongs to a pair of surfaces, not to a single object. A ball colliding with two different surfaces has two different e values.

Three formal definitions exist in the literature, which agree for simple direct (head-on, frictionless) impacts like this simulation but can differ for oblique, rough or eccentric impacts:

Kinematic (Newton): Ratio of relative velocities after/before impact — the one used in this simulation.
Kinetic (Poisson): Ratio of the impulse during the restitution phase to the impulse during the compression (deformation) phase.
Energetic (Stronge): Defined through the work done by the contact force during restitution vs. compression, i.e. \(e^2 = -W_{\text{restitution}} / W_{\text{compression}}\).

Energy Loss per Bounce

Kinetic energy scales with \(v^2\), so the fraction of kinetic energy kept in a single impact is \(e^2\):

\[ \frac{KE_{\text{after}}}{KE_{\text{before}}} = e^2 \]

Fraction of the original energy remaining after n bounces, and the percentage lost:

\[ \frac{KE_n}{KE_0} = e^{2n} \qquad \text{Energy lost} = (1 - e^{2n}) \times 100\% \]

Physically, the "lost" energy is not destroyed; it is converted to heat, sound, and internal vibrations / plastic deformation of the ball and surface. This simulation is deliberately idealised (no air drag, e constant) so the only dissipation is the factor e at each impact.

Example: For \(e=0.8\), each bounce keeps 64% of the energy (\(0.8^2 = 0.64\)) and reaches 64% of the previous height.

Factors That Affect e

  • Materials of BOTH bodies: e is a property of the pair (e.g. the same ball bounces differently on steel, wood, and carpet).
  • Impact velocity: e generally decreases as impact speed rises, because higher speeds cause more plastic deformation. Sports-ball drop tests show measured COR falling as drop height (and therefore impact velocity) increases. Example: a steel ball at ~1 m/s has \(e \approx 0.65\), dropping to about 0.55 at ~10 m/s.
  • Temperature: For rubber and similar polymers, resilience (and therefore e) rises with temperature over roughly the 0°C to 100°C range; a cold rubber ball bounces noticeably less. Some metals become more brittle at very low temperatures, lowering e.
  • Shape, size and construction: Multi-layer balls (e.g. golf balls with a core, mantle and cover) are engineered so their COR meets standards.
  • Spin and angle of impact: This lab models a purely vertical, non-spinning drop; oblique impacts only apply e to the velocity component along the line of impact (normal to the surface).

Note: Because of velocity dependence, the constant e used in this simulation is an idealisation valid over a modest range of impact speeds.

Total Distance and Total Time

The ball bounces infinitely many times but comes to rest in a finite time and travels a finite distance, because the bounce heights and durations form geometric series with ratio less than 1.

Total vertical distance travelled (drop + all up-and-down segments):

\[ D = h_0 + 2h_0\left(e^2 + e^4 + e^6 + \dots\right) = h_0 \frac{1+e^2}{1-e^2} \]

Total time until the ball comes to rest:

\[ T = \sqrt{\frac{2h_0}{g}} \cdot \frac{1+e}{1-e} \]

Derivation hint: The first fall takes \(t_0 = \sqrt{2h_0/g}\), and every later rise-and-fall takes \(2 t_0 e^n\) for bounce n, so \(T = t_0\left(1 + 2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} e^n\right)\).

Verified Example: For \(h_0 = 1\text{ m}\), \(e = 0.5\), \(g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2\) the ball travels a total of 1.667 m (≈1.67 m) and comes to rest after 1.355 s (≈1.36 s).

Real-World Applications

  • Sports: Tennis, golf, cricket, basketball and football governing bodies specify ball COR/bounce limits so equipment plays consistently (e.g. golf balls must meet a maximum initial-velocity standard that is closely tied to core COR).
  • Vehicle crash analysis: Restitution determines how much relative velocity is recovered after impact.
  • Engineering & robotics: Modelling impacts in machines, legged robots and packaging/drop-test design.
  • Safety: Playground surfaces and protective equipment are designed for low e (energy absorbing).

Limitations of the Model

  • Assumes e is constant, but real e varies with impact velocity and temperature.
  • Ignores air resistance (small for dense balls at low speed, but significant for light balls dropped from large heights).
  • Models only vertical, frictionless, non-rotating impacts.
  • Treats the ball as a point particle: no contact-time, no vibration of the ball or floor, and assumes the floor has effectively infinite mass.
  • Real balls eventually stop bouncing after a finite number of visible bounces once the rebound height falls below the surface roughness / resolution scale, even though the ideal maths predicts infinitely many.
